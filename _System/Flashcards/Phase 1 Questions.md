## 1.1 [[1.1 Think Like a Trader|Think Like a Trader]]

Your system has a real edge, but you lose the next 5 trades. What does that tell you about the system?
?
Almost nothing: 5 trades is far too small a sample. Losing streaks are normal inside winning systems (see the figure in section 3).

Write a complete edge in three lines for a trade you've seen or read about.
?
For example. **Setup:** uptrend on the daily, price pulls back to a support zone. **Trigger:** a bullish engulfing candle closes in the zone. **Management:** stop below the candle's low, target at the previous high.

You entered without a stop, the trade went against you, then came back and closed at +2R. Which box is it, and what do you log?
?
Bad process + good outcome ("dumb luck", the dangerous box). Log it as a mistake so the habit isn't rewarded.

A system wins 35% of trades with wins of +3R and losses of −1R. What's its expectancy, and is it worth trading?
?
0.35 × 3 − 0.65 × 1 = 1.05 − 0.65 = **+0.40R** per trade. Yes, if it holds over a large sample, even though it loses most trades.

Why is "bad process, good outcome" the most dangerous result?
?
It rewards breaking the rules, so you're likely to repeat it, and over many trades that habit loses money.


## 1.2 [[1.2 How Markets Move|How Markets Move]]

"Price went up because there were more buyers than sellers." What's wrong with this sentence, and how would you fix it?
?
Every trade has one buyer and one seller, so the numbers are always equal. Better: "Price went up because buyers were more aggressive: they kept paying the ask until sellers were found at higher prices."

Bid 1.0850, ask 1.0852 on EUR/USD. What's the spread in pips, and what does a 1-lot trade pay for it?
?
1.0852 − 1.0850 = 0.0002 = **2 pips**; at 10 USD per pip per lot = **20 USD**.

You want to buy only if price falls back to 1,950 support. Which order type do you use, and what's the risk?
?
A **limit buy** at 1,950. You get your price, but if price never comes back, you're not filled.

Why do big players care where stop losses sit?
?
Stops become market orders when hit. A cluster of sell stops is a pool of market sell orders that a big buyer can buy from (liquidity).

Name the three causes of a change in urgency from section 5, with one example each.
?
New information (an inflation release), forced flows (stop losses or margin calls being triggered), liquidity gaps (a thin book at night lets a small order move price a lot).

The book shows 100 for sale at 50.10 and 400 at 50.12. You buy 300 at market. What's your average price?
?
(100 × 50.10 + 200 × 50.12) ÷ 300 = (5,010 + 10,024) ÷ 300 = **50.1133**.

Explain in one sentence why a stop loss is "a market order waiting to happen".
?
When price touches the stop level, the stop becomes a market order and fills at the best available price, not necessarily at the stop price.


## 1.3 [[1.3 Candlestick Anatomy|Candlestick Anatomy]]

A candle has open 50, high 53, low 49, close 52. Draw it in words: colour, body size, each wick.
?
Green (close 52 > open 50). Body 52 − 50 = **2**. Upper wick 53 − 52 = **1**. Lower wick 50 − 49 = **1**.

O 80, H 81, L 74, C 75. What's the close location, and who won?
?
(75 − 74) ÷ (81 − 74) = 1 ÷ 7 ≈ **14%**: bottom 25%. Red candle, sellers won and closed near the low.

Five green candles in a row, each body smaller than the last and with growing upper wicks. What is the market telling you?
?
The push up is losing energy: buyers are still trying, but each attempt is rejected higher up. It's a warning, not yet a reversal.

Why is the close the most important of the four prices?
?
It's where the fight ended: the price both sides accepted at the end of the period. Highs and lows were only visited.

A candle has a big red body and a close location of 5%. Is the minute before the close a good moment to judge it? Why or why not?
?
No: until the candle closes, its shape can still change completely. Judge candles only after they close.


## 1.4 [[1.4 Candlestick Patterns in Context|Candlestick Patterns in Context]]

A candle's whole range sits inside the previous candle's range. What is it called, and how is it traded?
?
An inside bar: compression. You trade the break of its high or low, ideally in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend.

Why does the same hammer work at support and fail in the middle of a downtrend?
?
At support there are resting buy orders and sellers taking profit, so someone has a reason to defend that price. In the middle of nowhere there are no special orders to react.

A clean bullish engulfing forms at a weekly support zone, but the daily trend is strongly down. How many filters does it pass, and what do you do?
?
Location ✓ and quality ✓, but context ✗: two filters = **watch**, don't trade yet.

List the three filters in order and how many must pass before you trade.
?
Location, context, quality. All **three** must pass; two = watch; one = ignore.

Entry 2,010, stop 2,000, target 2,035. What's the R:R, and does it pass the checklist rule?
?
Risk 10, reward 25 → R:R = **2.5**. Yes: it's at least 2× the risk.


## 1.5 [[1.5 Timeframes & Top-Down Analysis|Timeframes & Top-Down Analysis]]

A 1-hour candle is green with a long lower wick. What do its four 15-minute candles probably look like?
?
Price fell early in the hour (red 15m candle(s) making the low), then recovered (green 15m candles), closing near the top. Same data, more detail.

Is 1H → 4H → Daily a sensible set of three? Check the ratios.
?
4H ÷ 1H = **4×**; Daily ÷ 4H = **6×** (24 hours ÷ 4). Both are within the 4–6× rule, so yes.

Put these in order and name each step: "bullish engulfing on 1H", "daily uptrend", "4H pullback into demand".
?
Daily uptrend (**direction**) → 4H pullback into demand (**location**) → bullish engulfing on 1H (**trigger**).

The daily trend is down, and the 15m chart shows a strong bounce. What does the table say to do?
?
Don't buy the bounce. Wait for it to fail at resistance, then look for sells in the daily direction.

Name the three steps of top-down analysis and which timeframe each uses.
?
Direction on the HTF, location on the MTF, trigger on the LTF.

Entry 50, target 56 from the HTF. A 15m trigger allows a stop at 49; a 4H trigger needs a stop at 47. What's the R:R of each?
?
15m: 6 ÷ 1 = **6**. 4H: 6 ÷ 3 = **2**.


## 1.6 [[1.6 Checkpoint- Phase 1 Review|Checkpoint: Phase 1 Review]]

A system wins 40% of trades: average win +2.5R, average loss −1R. What's the expectancy, and what would you expect over 50 trades?
?
0.4 × 2.5 − 0.6 × 1 = 1.0 − 0.6 = **+0.4R** per trade → about **+20R** over 50 trades (with long losing streaks along the way, see 1.1).

The book has 200 for sale at 10.00 and 300 at 10.05. You buy 400 at market. What's your average price, and how much slippage did you pay versus 10.00?
?
200 × 10.00 + 200 × 10.05 = 4,010 → average **10.025**; slippage = 4,010 − 4,000 = **10** (see 1.2).

O 120, H 121, L 112, C 120.5. Colour, body, lower wick and close location?
?
Green; body **0.5**; lower wick 120 − 112 = **8**; close location (120.5 − 112) ÷ 9 ≈ **94%**: a strong hammer shape. It still needs a level and context to be a setup (see 1.3, 1.4).

Daily uptrend. Entry 200, target 212. A 1H trigger allows a stop at 197; a daily trigger needs 190. With 60 USD risk, what's the R:R and size of each?
?
1H: risk 3 → R:R **4**, size 60 ÷ 3 = **20 units**. Daily: risk 10 → R:R **1.2**, size **6 units** (see 1.5).

You bought a hammer in the middle of a downtrend without a stop, and it made +3R. Grade the trade and say what you log.
?
Bad process (no level, against context, no stop) + good outcome. Log it as a **mistake**: it's the "dumb luck" box from 1.1.

In one line each: what does the HTF decide, what does the MTF decide, what does the LTF decide?
?
HTF: direction. MTF: location (the level to trade from). LTF: the trigger and a tight, logical stop.
