## 8.1 [[8.1 Options Basics- Calls & Puts|Options Basics: Calls & Puts]]

You pay 2 for a call with strike 50. What is the most you can lose?
?
2 per share (200 USD for one 100-share contract). A buyer's loss is limited to the premium.

Who has the bigger risk: the call **buyer** or the call **seller**?
?
The seller. The buyer can only lose the premium; the seller must deliver at the strike however high the price goes.

Put with strike 100, premium 3. What is the break-even?
?
97 (strike − premium). Below 97 the put makes money at expiry.

Stock at 50. Is a put with strike 55 ITM or OTM? What is its intrinsic value?
?
ITM. The right to sell at 55 when the price is 50 is worth 5 now: intrinsic value = 5.


## 8.2 [[8.2 The Greeks|The Greeks]]

A put has delta −0.40. The stock rises by 2. Roughly what happens to the put's price?
?
It falls about 0.80 (−0.40 × 2), i.e. about −80 USD per contract.

Which Greek is your enemy if you buy an option and the market goes quiet for a week?
?
Theta: time decay eats the option's value every day.

Fill in: "Delta is ___, gamma is ___, theta is ___, vega is ___" using the car analogy.
?
Speed, acceleration, fuel leak, weather sensitivity.

Why do dealers care about gamma more than you do?
?
Because gamma tells them how much their hedge must change for every move. Big gamma = big, frequent hedging trades, which move the market (8.3).


## 8.3 [[8.3 Market Makers & Delta Hedging|Market Makers & Delta Hedging]]

A dealer is short puts and the market falls. Do they buy or sell futures to stay hedged?
?
Sell. As price falls, the short puts gain (negative) delta for the customer, which means the dealer becomes *longer* the market; to get back to neutral they sell futures, selling into the decline.

Why doesn't the dealer just keep the position unhedged?
?
Because they'd be making a directional bet. Their business is earning the spread on huge volumes, not guessing direction.

In one sentence each: what does a long-gamma dealer do on a rally, and what does a short-gamma dealer do?
?
Long gamma sells into the rally (calming it). Short gamma buys into the rally (fuelling it).


## 8.4 [[8.4 Gamma Exposure (GEX) & The Gamma Flip|Gamma Exposure (GEX) & The Gamma Flip]]

Same strike, but open interest doubles to 140,000 contracts. What happens to its GEX?
?
It doubles to about +$6.6bn. GEX scales directly with open interest.

Why are puts counted as negative?
?
Because the model assumes customers *buy* puts (for protection), so dealers are *short* them, which means short gamma: their hedging goes *with* the move.

The index is 30 points below the flip and VIX is rising. What kind of day should you plan for?
?
A negative-gamma, volatile day: expect bigger ranges and trends, respect breakouts, reduce position size.

A friend says "GEX is positive, so the market will go up." What is wrong?
?
GEX is about *volatility*, not direction. Positive GEX means moves tend to be dampened in both directions.


## 8.5 [[8.5 Call Walls & Put Walls|Call Walls & Put Walls]]

On day 3 in the figure, a trade happened but open interest didn't change. Why?
?
Ann sold her existing contract to Eve: one person closed, another opened, so the number of open contracts stayed the same.

Why can the put wall turn from a floor into an accelerator?
?
If dealers are short those puts (short gamma), once price breaks below the strike, their hedging means selling more as price falls, which pushes the drop further.

The call wall moved from 5,100 to 5,200 overnight while price rose. What probably happened?
?
Traders rolled their calls higher (closed 5,100, opened 5,200), so the ceiling moved up with the market.


## 8.6 [[8.6 Vanna (VEX) & Charm|Vanna (VEX) & Charm]]

Which Greek describes delta changing as **time** passes, and which one as **volatility** changes?
?
Time: charm. Volatility: vanna.

If customers mostly hold **calls** instead of puts and dealers are short them, what does falling IV do to dealers' hedges?
?
OTM call deltas shrink too, so dealers who were long futures to hedge short calls would **sell** some of them back. The direction of the flow depends on who holds what.

Why is the week **after** monthly OPEX often called a "window of weakness"?
?
The expiring options and their charm/vanna support disappear, so the steady dealer buying stops, and the market is more exposed to new selling.

Customers are long puts and dealers hedge with short futures. What happens to dealer flow as (a) time passes and (b) IV falls?
?
In both cases put deltas shrink, dealers need fewer short futures, and they buy futures back: supportive flow.

Using the walkthrough, how many futures do dealers buy back when a 10,000-put position's delta goes from −0.30 to −0.20?
?
About 1,000 (10,000 × 0.10).


## 8.7 [[8.7 Reading Options Flow|Reading Options Flow]]

A strike has OI 5,000 and today's volume is 1,200. Can you tell whether the trades were opening or closing?
?
Not today. All 1,200 could be closing. Check tomorrow's OI: if it rises, some were opening; if it falls, most were closing.

A $2M put block prints **at the bid**, volume 3,000 vs OI 20,000. Bearish bet?
?
Probably not. At the bid means the seller was aggressive, and volume < OI means it may be closing. Most likely someone sold or closed puts.

Which four things do you check on a print before giving it any weight?
?
Side (at ask vs at bid), size in premium, volume vs open interest (opening?), and context (expiry, part of a spread or hedge, upcoming events).

Can a single large aggressive call sweep be a reason to buy the stock?
?
No. At most it raises conviction in a setup you already have (structure, levels, trigger). Never trade because of one print.


## 8.8 [[8.8 Checkpoint- Phase 8 Review|Checkpoint: Phase 8 Review]]

You buy a 4,900 put on an index for 35 points. At expiry the index is at 4,820, or at 4,880. Result per point in each case, and the break-even?
?
At 4,820: intrinsic 80 − 35 = **+45**. At 4,880: intrinsic 20 − 35 = **−15**. Break-even = 4,900 − 35 = **4,865** (see 8.1).

A call has delta 0.40 and gamma 0.03. The stock rises 3. New delta? For 10 contracts (100 shares each), how many shares of exposure before and after?
?
Delta ≈ 0.40 + 0.03 × 3 = **0.49**. Exposure 10 × 100 × 0.40 = **400** shares → 10 × 100 × 0.49 = **490** shares (see 8.2).

Dealers are short 2,000 calls (100 shares each) with delta 0.50, hedged with stock. Price rises and the delta becomes 0.60. What must they do?
?
Hedge = 2,000 × 100 × 0.50 = **100,000** shares long; needed now 120,000 → **buy 20,000 more** into the rally. Short gamma means buying strength and selling weakness, which amplifies moves (see 8.3).

Above vs below the gamma flip: which tactics fit each?
?
Above (positive gamma): calmer, mean-reverting: fade the edges, take profits quickly. Below (negative gamma): faster, trending: smaller size, wider stops, follow breaks (see 8.4).

Why are GEX levels and options flow only context, never a trigger?
?
They are estimates built on assumptions (who is long or short, daily open interest) and don't give direction on their own; the trade still needs your structure setup and risk plan (see 8.4, 8.7).
