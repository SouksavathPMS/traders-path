## 3.1 [[3.1 Why Risk Comes First|Why Risk Comes First]]

Two traders use the same profitable system. One risks 1%, the other 10%. Why can the second one lose everything?
?
Losing streaks are certain. At 10% per trade, an ordinary 8-loss streak takes the account down about 57%; the deeper the hole, the harder the recovery, and many traders break their rules on the way.

With a 40% win rate, roughly how likely is a streak of 8+ losses within 100 trades?
?
About **49%**, roughly a coin flip (see the table above). Plan for it.

You have 1% open on each of three longs: two US tech stocks and the Nasdaq index. Your max open risk is 3%. Are you within the rule?
?
On paper yes (3%), but all three tend to move together, so it behaves like one 3% bet on tech. If your rule treats correlated trades as one, you're at the limit for a single idea.

Account 8,000 USD, risk 0.5%. What's your 1R, and what would 10 losses in a row cost (compounded)?
?
1R = **40 USD**. 0.995¹⁰ ≈ 0.951 → about **−4.9%** (≈ 390 USD).

Why does large risk per trade make a good system worse in practice, not better?
?
It doesn't change the edge, only the damage from streaks: deeper drawdowns, much larger gains needed to recover, and more emotional mistakes.


## 3.2 [[3.2 Position Sizing|Position Sizing]]

Account 5,000 USD, risk 1%, stop distance 0.80 per share. How many shares?
?
1R = 50 USD. 50 ÷ 0.80 = 62.5 → round down to 62 shares.

1R = 60 USD. EUR/USD stop 30 pips, 10 USD per pip per standard lot. What size?
?
Loss per lot = 30 × 10 = 300 USD. 60 ÷ 300 = 0.2 lots.

1R = 30 USD. You want to trade one full E-mini S&P (50 USD per point) with a 4-point stop. Can you?
?
No: one contract risks 4 × 50 = 200 USD, more than 1R. Use micro contracts (MES: 4 × 5 = 20 USD each → 1 contract) or skip.

Why can't a stop loss guarantee your maximum loss?
?
A stop is an order that becomes a market order when touched. If price gaps past it, it fills at the next available price, which can be much worse.

Write the position-size formula and say what each part means.
?
Size = (Account × Risk %) ÷ Stop distance per unit. Account × Risk % is 1R in money; the stop distance is how much one unit loses at the stop (plus costs).

Why do you always round down?
?
Rounding up quietly increases the risk above 1R; rounding down keeps the loss at or below your limit.


## 3.3 [[3.3 R-Multiples & Risk-Reward|R-Multiples & Risk:Reward]]

Long 32.00, stop 31.20, exit 34.10. Short 1.0850, stop 1.0880, exit 1.0790. R-multiples?
?
Long: 1R = 0.80; (34.10 − 32.00) ÷ 0.80 = **+2.63R**. Short: 1R = 30 pips; profit 60 pips → **+2R**.

You win 70% of trades, but your average win is 0.4R and your average loss is 1R. Profitable?
?
Break-even for 1:0.4 is 1 ÷ 1.4 ≈ **71%**. At 70%: 0.7 × 0.4 − 0.3 × 1 = **−0.02R** per trade, slightly losing.

Your structural stop is 2.0 away and the next resistance is 2.2 away. You want 1:3, so you set the target 6.0 away. What's wrong?
?
The target is invented. The honest R:R is about **1.1** (to the real obstacle). Skip the trade or find a better entry; don't stretch the target.

What's the break-even win rate for a planned 1:2.5 trade (before costs)?
?
1 ÷ 3.5 ≈ **29%**.

Why measure progress in R instead of in dollars?
?
R separates skill (the R your decisions produce) from size (how much each R is worth), so you can compare trades across markets and see whether you're improving even while your size changes.


## 3.4 [[3.4 Win Rate x R = Expectancy|Win Rate x R = Expectancy]]

Win rate 40%, average win 2.5R, average loss 1R. What's the expectancy, and is it above break-even?
?
0.40 × 2.5 − 0.60 × 1 = 1.00 − 0.60 = +0.40R. Break-even at 2.5R is 1 ÷ 3.5 ≈ 29%, so 40% is well above.

Your system shows −0.1R per trade after 15 trades. Should you abandon it?
?
Not on that evidence. 15 trades is far too few; a +0.26R system can easily be negative after 15 trades. Keep following the plan at small risk and re-check at 50 and 100.

Expectancy +0.3R, 10 trades a month, 0.5% risk per trade. Expected return per month?
?
0.3 × 10 = 3R → 3 × 0.5% = about +1.5% a month on average.

Write the expectancy formula and calculate it for 35% wins at 3R and 65% losses at 1R.
?
Expectancy = Win% × avg win − Loss% × avg loss = 0.35 × 3 − 0.65 × 1 = +0.40R.

What win rate do you need to break even with an average win of 1.5R and an average loss of 1R?
?
1 ÷ (1 + 1.5) = 40%.


## 3.5 [[3.5 Stop Loss & Take Profit Placement|Stop Loss & Take Profit Placement]]

Long on a 1H CHoCH. The swing low that created it is 48.20; ATR(14) on 1H is 0.60. Where's the stop with a 0.5 × ATR buffer?
?
48.20 − 0.30 = **47.90**.

The structural stop is 4.0 away instead of the usual 2.0. Your risk is 100 USD. What changes?
?
Only the size: 100 ÷ 4.0 = **25 units** instead of 50. Don't tighten the stop into the noise; if the R:R to the real target is now too low, skip the trade.

You move to break-even the moment a trade reaches +0.5R. What tends to happen, and what's a better rule?
?
Normal pullbacks stop you out at zero right before the move. Better: move to break-even only after a new swing in your favour (BOS) or at +1.5–2R.

Name the four prices to write before every entry.
?
Invalidation, stop (invalidation ± buffer), target(s), and the management rule (A, B or C, plus when to move to break-even).

Short at 75.40. The swing high is 76.10, ATR 0.80, buffer 0.25 × ATR. Where's the stop, and what's 1R per unit?
?
Stop = 76.10 + 0.20 = **76.30**. 1R = 76.30 − 75.40 = **0.90** per unit.


## 3.6 [[3.6 Drawdown Math & Recovery|Drawdown Math & Recovery]]

Your account falls from 5,000 to 3,500. What's the drawdown, and what gain do you need?
?
Drawdown = 1,500 ÷ 5,000 = **−30%**. Gain needed = 0.30 ÷ 0.70 ≈ **+42.9%**.

Your worst losing streak in a backtest is 9 trades. You risk 1%. Roughly what worst drawdown should you prepare for?
?
Streak ≈ 1 − 0.99⁹ ≈ **−8.6%**; times 1.5–2 → plan for about **−13% to −17%**.

You're 6% below your peak; your plan halves risk at −5%. A setup looks perfect. What size do you use?
?
The reduced size (e.g. 0.5%). The rule exists for exactly this moment; "this one looks perfect" is not an exception.

What gain is needed to recover from −20%, −50% and −75%?
?
**+25%**, **+100%**, **+300%**.

Why is cutting risk the right response both to bad luck and to a broken system?
?
If it's bad luck, it costs little because you scale back up at a new high. If the system or your execution is broken, it protects capital while you find the problem.


## 3.8 [[3.8 Trade Management- Partial Profits, Trailing Stops, Scaling In & Out|Trade Management: Partial Profits, Trailing Stops, Scaling In & Out]]

A friend says their trailing stop "made a losing system profitable". What should you ask?
?
How many trades, and whether the system had an edge to begin with. With no edge, exit methods average about zero; small samples can make any method look good.

Why does a 3R target win less often than a 1R target?
?
Price has to travel three times as far before hitting the stop, so the stop is hit first more often.

Entry 50, stop 48, 3 units. You sell 1 unit at 54, then the rest stops out at break-even. Result in R?
?
Risk = 3 × 2 = 6. Profit = 1 × 4 = 4 → 4 ÷ 6 ≈ **+0.67R**.

Long from 40, highest high 46, ATR 0.8. Where is a 2 × ATR chandelier stop?
?
46 − 1.6 = **44.4**.

Why is averaging down forbidden while scaling in is allowed?
?
Averaging down increases size on a trade that's proving you wrong, so the loss grows beyond 1R. Scaling in adds only when the trade is working and keeps the total worst case at 1R.

With no edge, what does the average result of any exit method tend towards?
?
About **0R** (minus costs). Management can't create an edge.

You hold 100 units from 20 (stop 19). Price is 23. You want to add 100 units at 23 and keep the total worst case at 1R (100 USD). Where must the common stop go?
?
Worst case: 100 × (s − 20) + 100 × (s − 23) = −100 → 200s − 4,300 = −100 → s = **21.0**.


## 3.9 [[3.9 Portfolio Risk- Correlation, Open Risk, Kelly & Monte Carlo Drawdowns|Portfolio Risk: Correlation, Open Risk, Kelly & Monte Carlo Drawdowns]]

You hold 1% risk each on long Nasdaq futures, long Apple and long Nvidia. Your limit is 3% total and 2% per theme. Are you within your rules?
?
Total 3% is within the limit, but all three are one "US tech" theme at 3% > 2%. Close or reduce one.

Why does correlation matter less for positions that are truly unrelated?
?
Their losses don't usually happen at the same time, so the combined swing grows like the square root of the number of positions, not in a straight line.

Win rate 40%, average win 1.5R. Kelly fraction?
?
0.40 − 0.60 ÷ 1.5 = 0.40 − 0.40 = **0**: no edge, so Kelly says bet nothing.

Why does growth fall again when you bet more than Kelly?
?
Losses compound against a bigger stake: a few losses shrink the account so much that later wins can't make it back.

In the default run, what is the chance of a drawdown of 20% or more at 1% risk? And at 2%?
?
**0.7%** at 1% risk; **35.6%** at 2% risk (`--risk 2`).

Why run thousands of simulations instead of looking at your backtest's one equity curve?
?
A backtest is one possible order of trades. Monte Carlo shows the range of drawdowns and outcomes the same edge can produce, including bad luck you haven't seen yet.

Win rate 50%, average win 2R. Kelly fraction and a sensible quarter-Kelly?
?
0.50 − 0.50 ÷ 2 = **0.25** (25%); quarter Kelly = **6.25%**, still far above the 0.5–1% this course uses.

Three positions of 1% each with correlation 1 between all of them. How big is the combined swing?
?
√(3 + 6 × 1) = √9 = **3%**: exactly like one 3% position.


## 3.7 [[3.7 Checkpoint- Phase 3 Review|Checkpoint: Phase 3 Review]]

Account 15,000 USD, risk 0.75%. Long a stock at 84.20 with a stop at 82.60. How many shares?
?
1R = 15,000 × 0.0075 = **112.50 USD**. Risk per share 1.60 → 112.50 ÷ 1.60 = 70.3 → **70 shares** (actual risk 112 USD) (see 3.2).

38% win rate, average win +2.4R, average loss −1.05R. Expectancy, and the result of 100 trades at 1R = 100 USD?
?
0.38 × 2.4 − 0.62 × 1.05 = 0.912 − 0.651 = **+0.26R** per trade → about **+26R ≈ +2,610 USD** over 100 trades (see 3.4).

Equity peak 12,400 USD, account now 11,000 USD. Your rules: halve at −5%, stop and review at −10%. What's the drawdown, and what do you do?
?
11,000 ÷ 12,400 − 1 ≈ **−11.3%** → **stop live trading and review**. You need +12.7% to get back to the peak (see 3.6).

Short EUR/USD at 1.0880. Swing high 1.0912, ATR 0.0020, buffer 0.25 × ATR. Risk 50 USD, 10 USD per pip per lot. Stop and size?
?
Stop = 1.0912 + 0.0005 = **1.0917** → 37 pips. Size = 50 ÷ (37 × 10) = 0.135 → **0.13 lot** (risk ≈ 48 USD) (see 3.5).

Why must you take at least 30 trades with one exit method before judging it?
?
A handful of trades is mostly luck; streaks of 6–8 losses are normal. Only a larger sample gives an expectancy you can trust (and 100+ is better).

In one line each: what decides the stop, the target and the size?
?
Stop: the structure that proves the idea wrong, plus a buffer. Target: the next real opposing level. Size: 1R ÷ stop distance.
