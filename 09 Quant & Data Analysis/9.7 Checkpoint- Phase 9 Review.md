# Checkpoint: Phase 9 Review

> [!mindset]
> Phase 9 is the referee for everything before it. Liquidity, Fibonacci, Elliott, orderflow, GEX: each is a hypothesis until **your data** says otherwise. If you leave this phase with one habit, make it this one: *every claim gets a sample size and an out-of-sample test.*

![[p9-summary.en.svg]]

## Key takeaways

| Lesson | The one thing to remember |
|---|---|
| 9.1 Statistics | Mean, median, σ, fat tails. With 20 trades, a 50% win rate could be 28–72%. |
| 9.2 Probability & EV | EV = Σ p × outcome, all branches and costs. Conditions change probabilities. |
| 9.3 Backtesting | No look-ahead, costs, survivorship, out-of-sample, walk-forward. Overfitting picks luck. |
| 9.4 Internals | $TICK, $ADD, $VOLD, VIX confirm or question an index move. |
| 9.5 Python strategy | Simple rules, honest test. The in-sample winner lagged out-of-sample. |
| 9.6 Metrics | Read Sharpe, Sortino, drawdown, profit factor *together*, always with the trade count. |

## Quiz

```quiz
Q: A new setup shows a 60% win rate after 15 trades. What's the best conclusion?
- It's clearly better than a 45% setup
* Too few trades: the true win rate could plausibly be anywhere from ~35% to ~85%
- It will stay at 60%
E: ±1.96 × √(0.6 × 0.4 ÷ 15) ≈ ±25 percentage points.
Q: A trade has: 40% chance of +2R, 20% chance of +0.5R, 40% chance of −1R. What is the EV?
* +0.50R
- +0.10R
- −0.10R
E: 0.4 × 2 + 0.2 × 0.5 − 0.4 × 1 = 0.8 + 0.1 − 0.4 = +0.5R.
Q: After five losses in a row on a 50% win-rate system, the chance the next trade wins is…
- Higher than 50%: a win is due
* Still about 50%, if trades are independent
- Lower than 50%
E: Believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy.
Q: A backtest buys at the close of the bar that generated the signal, using that bar's close in the signal. Which bias is this?
* Look-ahead bias
- Survivorship bias
- Data snooping
E: The signal uses information that isn't final until that same close. Execute on the next bar.
Q: You test 200 parameter combinations and keep the best. Its in-sample Sharpe is 1.8. What should you expect out-of-sample?
- About 1.8
* Much lower, possibly near zero: the best of many is partly luck
- Higher, because it's optimised
E: Overfitting and data snooping inflate in-sample results.
Q: What does walk-forward testing do?
- Tests the strategy on the most recent data only
* Optimises on a rolling window and tests on the next unseen window, repeatedly
- Removes the need for costs
E: Stitched test windows approximate live trading.
Q: The index breaks to a new high, but $ADD is negative and $VOLD is falling. What does it suggest?
- Broad, healthy breakout
* Narrow participation: be suspicious of the breakout
- Nothing; internals don't matter for indices
E: Breadth that doesn't confirm price often precedes failed auctions.
Q: Strategy A: Sharpe 0.9, max DD −12%, 180 trades. Strategy B: Sharpe 1.4, max DD −40%, 14 trades. Which result is more trustworthy?
* A: more trades and smaller drawdown
- B: higher Sharpe
- Equal
E: B's Sharpe rests on 14 trades and came with a −40% drawdown.
Q: A system's profit factor is 12 from 3 trades. What does it mean?
- An exceptional edge
* Almost nothing: the sample is far too small
- The system can't lose
E: Profit factor needs many trades to be meaningful.
Q: In the 9.5 backtest, what was the strategy's clearest genuine benefit out-of-sample?
- Higher return than buy & hold
* Lower volatility and drawdown
- More trades
E: It lagged on return and Sharpe but reduced risk. That's typical of trend filters.
```

## Self-assessment


> [!check]- Worked problems: Phase 9
> **Q1.** A setup wins 22 of 40 trades (55%). Roughly what range could the true win rate be in (95%)?
> > [!answer]-
> > ± 1.96 × √(0.55 × 0.45 ÷ 40) ≈ ±0.15 → about **40% to 70%**. Not yet enough to call it better than 50% (see 9.1).
> **Q2.** A trade has a 35% chance of +2.5R, 25% chance of +0.3R and 40% chance of −1R, with costs of 0.05R per trade. EV?
> > [!answer]-
> > 0.35 × 2.5 + 0.25 × 0.3 − 0.40 × 1 − 0.05 = 0.875 + 0.075 − 0.40 − 0.05 = **+0.50R** (see 9.2).
> **Q3.** 80 trades: 30 winners averaging +1.8R, 50 losers at −1R. Profit factor and expectancy?
> > [!answer]-
> > Gross wins 54R, gross losses 50R → profit factor **1.08**; expectancy (54 − 50) ÷ 80 = **+0.05R**: barely positive, and costs could erase it (see 9.6).

> [!check]- Final check: Phase 9
> **Q1.** Name three ways a backtest can lie, and the fix for each.
> > [!answer]-
> > For example: look-ahead bias (only use data available at the decision time); ignoring costs (include spread, commission, slippage); overfitting (keep an out-of-sample period, use walk-forward) (see 9.3).
> **Q2.** Why must every performance metric come with a trade count?
> > [!answer]-
> > Small samples give extreme, unreliable numbers (e.g. profit factor 12 from 3 trades). The trade count tells you how much luck could be in the result.
